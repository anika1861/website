Moving site files out of a war zone

IQVIA, 2022. Crisis leadership and business continuity.

24 h

to formal sponsor approval, with zero data loss

Situation

When the war in Ukraine broke out, several of our sites were in the affected area. The original investigator site files, and with them the integrity of the trial data, were at risk. The sponsor had no emergency archiving vendor in place.

What I did

  1. Raised the risk with the sponsor and proposed a pre-approved, audited archiving provider from IQVIA’s network.
  2. Presented a complete logistics plan, the provider’s capabilities and a cost estimate, and secured formal sponsor approval within 24 hours.
  3. Coordinated the provider and the sites as the situation changed. The files went first to a secure warehouse in Kyiv, then to a long-term secure facility in Poland.

Result

All essential documents were secured with zero data loss, and the sponsor formally recognised the team for its risk management under extreme conditions.

Catching a budget overrun early

IQVIA. Lab-data integrity and financial forecasting.

18%

projected budget overrun, caught and averted

Situation

On a global study, monthly actuals tracked the plan. My bottom-up estimate at completion (EAC) told a different story: the study was heading for an 18% budget overrun.

What I did

  1. Ran a variance analysis that isolated the problem to the central lab vendor, where frequent unbudgeted, out-of-scope work was building a large future liability.
  2. Found the root cause: a high rate of haemolysed blood samples, mostly from Eastern European sites. Haemolysis is a pre-analytical artefact that distorts safety labs such as potassium and AST, and every spoiled sample triggered an expensive re-collection.
  3. Worked with the vendor to trace it to centrifugation at the sites and temperature swings in transit, then rolled out targeted site retraining and better-insulated shipping.

Result

Haemolysis fell to almost zero and the unbudgeted costs stopped. The re-forecast EAC returned to a 3–5% variance, and the study finished under its approved budget.

The study’s first patient worldwide

Alexion. Rare disease study start-up.

3,000 km

between the first patient and the only eligible diagnostic centre

Situation

During start-up of a rare disease study we found that none of our selected sites had the diagnostic equipment the protocol required for eligibility. Without it, the study could not run in our country.

What I did

  1. Confirmed the scale of the problem: only one facility in the whole country had the equipment.
  2. Mapped the distances. The facility was thousands of kilometres from our main site, and the first candidate patient lived more than 2,000 km from that site and 3,000 km from the diagnostic centre.
  3. Brought the feasibility, logistics and contracts leads together into one team with a cross-functional plan.
  4. Ran the work in parallel. Contracts drafted agreements with the diagnostic facility in record time while logistics and I planned flights, hotels and ground transport for the patient and their caregiver.

Result

We screened the first patient, and our country became the first in the world to enrol a patient in the study.
